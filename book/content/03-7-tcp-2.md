### Flow control: protecting the receiver

*Flow control* stops a fast sender from overrunning a slow receiver. The receiver advertises in every segment how many more bytes its buffer can take, the *receive window* (rwnd), and the sender never has more than that many bytes unacknowledged. Because the sender may transmit a whole window before it needs an acknowledgement, the scheme is termed a *sliding window*: the left edge advances with each acknowledgement, and the right edge advances with it.

If the receiver's buffer fills completely it advertises a window of zero and the sender stops. To avoid deadlock should the window update be lost, the sender then sends tiny *window probes* on a *persist timer*, with growing intervals. A related fault is the *silly window syndrome*, in which a slow receiver advertises tiny windows and a sender fills them with tiny segments, so that headers dominate: the receiver should not advertise a window until it can offer a full segment or half its buffer, and the sender should not send tiny segments when it can wait.

*Nagle's algorithm* is the sender-side rule. While any sent data is unacknowledged, a small write is held back and merged with later writes until the acknowledgement arrives or a full segment accumulates. This saves many small packets, but combined with the receiver's delayed ACK it can stall an interactive application: if the program writes a request in two small pieces, the second piece waits for the ACK of the first, and the ACK waits, for up to 40 to 200 ms, for the receiver to delay it. The usual fix is to disable Nagle with the socket option `TCP_NODELAY`, or to assemble the request in one write.

### Throughput limits and the window scale

Since at most one window is in flight per round trip, a connection cannot exceed throughput ≤ W / RTT, where W is the smaller of the windows. The window needed to keep a path full is the *bandwidth-delay product* (BDP), the capacity R times the round-trip time: the amount of data "in the pipe". The 16-bit window field holds at most 65,535 bytes, which the *window scale* option multiplies by 2<sup>s</sup>, with s up to 14, giving windows to about 1 GiB.

::: example
**A 64 KB window on a 100 ms path; then a 1 Gb/s path of the same length.**

1. Ceiling with W = 65,535 B and RTT = 100 ms: 65,535 × 8 / 0.1 = 5.24 Mb/s, whatever the link speed.
2. For a 1 Gb/s path, BDP = 10⁹ × 0.1 / 8 = 12.5 MB.
3. Required scale: 65,535 × 2<sup>s</sup> ≥ 12.5 × 10⁶, so 2<sup>s</sup> ≥ 191, so s = 8 (2⁸ = 256 gives 16.8 MB; s = 7 gives only 8.4 MB).
4. For a 1 Gb/s path with RTT 190 ms (Mumbai to Virginia): BDP = 23.75 MB, so s = 9 (33.5 MB).

[Result: 5.24 Mb/s without scaling; a shift of 8 for the 100 ms path and 9 for the 190 ms path. The receive buffer, not just the option, must be that large.]{.result}
:::

### Congestion control: protecting the network

*Flow control* protects the receiver; *congestion control* protects the network. In October 1986 the early Internet suffered *congestion collapse*: when queues filled, packets were dropped, senders retransmitted, the retransmissions filled the queues further, and throughput fell by a factor of a thousand. Van Jacobson's remedy, adopted in 1988, is that the sender keeps a second window, the *congestion window* (cwnd), which it grows when things go well and cuts when loss signals trouble. The sender may have at most min(cwnd, rwnd) bytes in flight. Loss is treated as the signal that a queue somewhere has overflowed, and cwnd is counted here in segments (MSS) for clarity.

The algorithm has two phases. In *slow start* cwnd starts small (1 segment in the classical description, 10 in modern stacks) and increases by one segment for every ACK, which doubles it every round trip, an exponential growth that is "slow" only compared with sending a whole window immediately. When cwnd reaches the *slow-start threshold* ssthresh, *congestion avoidance* begins, and cwnd grows by one segment per round trip, a linear increase. On loss the window is cut. This pattern, *additive increase, multiplicative decrease* (AIMD), is what lets many flows share a link fairly: each increase is equal, but each cut removes a share proportional to the flow's size, so unequal flows converge.

The variants differ in how they react to loss. *Tahoe*, the original, treats every loss alike: ssthresh = cwnd / 2, cwnd = 1, slow start again. *Reno* distinguishes the signals. A timeout is serious, so it acts like Tahoe. Three duplicate ACKs mean that later segments are still arriving, so the network is not dead: Reno sets ssthresh = cwnd / 2 and cwnd = ssthresh + 3 (the three duplicate ACKs each mark a segment that has left the network), retransmits the lost segment, and inflates cwnd by one per further duplicate ACK; when the new ACK arrives, cwnd deflates to ssthresh and congestion avoidance continues. This is *fast recovery*. *NewReno* improves recovery when several segments of one window are lost: it stays in fast recovery until all of them are acknowledged, treating a *partial ACK* as a sign of the next hole.

::: example
**Trace cwnd (in segments) with initial cwnd = 1 and ssthresh = 16, when loss is detected at cwnd = 20.**

1. Slow start, one value per RTT: RTT 1 to 5 give 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. At 16 = ssthresh the phase changes.
2. Congestion avoidance, +1 per RTT: RTT 6 to 9 give 17, 18, 19, 20.
3. Loss at cwnd = 20, so ssthresh = 20 / 2 = 10.
4. *Tahoe* (and any variant after a timeout): cwnd = 1, then slow start 1, 2, 4, 8, then 10 (capped at ssthresh), then 11, 12, 13 and so on.
5. *Reno* after three duplicate ACKs: cwnd = 10 + 3 = 13 during recovery, then 10 when the new ACK arrives, and then 11, 12, 13 ... in congestion avoidance.

[Result: ssthresh = 10 in both cases; Tahoe restarts from 1 and needs four extra round trips to reach the threshold, while Reno resumes at 10. Reno halves; a timeout resets to 1. Do not conflate the two.]{.result}
:::

Both Tahoe and Reno are loss-based and increase by one segment per round trip, which is too timid on a long fast path, where thousands of round trips are needed to recover after a single loss. Two modern algorithms address this in different ways.

*CUBIC*, the default in Linux (and in Windows and macOS), replaces the linear increase by a cubic function of the time since the last loss: W(t) = C (t − K)<sup>3</sup> + W<sub>max</sub>, where W<sub>max</sub> is the window when the loss occurred and K = (W<sub>max</sub> β / C)<sup>1/3</sup> is the time needed to climb back to it. After a loss the window is cut to 0.7 W<sub>max</sub>; it then rises quickly (the concave part), flattens around W<sub>max</sub> where the last loss occurred, and finally accelerates again to look for more capacity (the convex part). Since the function depends on time and not on the number of ACKs, flows with different round-trip times grow at the same rate, which is fairer than Reno.

*BBR* (bottleneck bandwidth and round-trip propagation time), from Google, drops the idea that loss is the signal. It builds a model of the path from two measurements: the maximum delivery rate seen recently, BtlBw, and the minimum round-trip time, RTprop. It then *paces* packets at about BtlBw and limits the data in flight to a small multiple of the BDP = BtlBw × RTprop, thus operating at the corner of the figure below, with a full pipe and an empty queue, instead of filling the buffer as loss-based algorithms do. Its phases are Startup (exponential search for BtlBw), Drain, ProbeBW (a cycle of eight round-trip phases with gains 1.25, 0.75 and six of 1, to detect more bandwidth) and ProbeRTT (a brief reduction every ten seconds to measure RTprop). BBR performs well on paths with random loss, where CUBIC collapses, and keeps queues short. Its first version, however, ignores loss and can take an unfair share when sharing a shallow buffer with CUBIC flows, which is why versions 2 and 3 add responses to loss and to ECN marks.


**What you should notice:** with 1% loss the Mathis estimate for a Reno-like flow, (MSS / RTT) × 1.22 / √p = (11,680 bit / 0.05 s) × 12.2, is about 2.9 Mb/s, and CUBIC lands in the same low range, whereas BBR is not governed by loss and reaches a large share of the veth capacity. Plot cwnd from repeated `ss -ti` samples to see CUBIC's sawtooth against BBR's flat, model-based window; set loss to 0 and watch the two converge. Exact numbers depend on the machine.
