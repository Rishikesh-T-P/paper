## [6.3]{.sec}802.11 architecture and the family tree [Must · ■□□]{.tag} {#s6-3}

IEEE 802.11 organises stations around an access point. A *station* (STA) is any device with an 802.11 radio. An *access point* (AP) is a station that also bridges to a wired network. The AP and the stations associated with it form a *basic service set* (BSS), the unit of radio coverage; the BSS is identified by a 48-bit *BSSID*, which is the MAC address of the AP's radio for that network. A human-readable network name, the *SSID* (up to 32 bytes), is advertised by the AP; several BSSs that carry the same SSID and are joined by a wired *distribution system* (DS) form an *extended service set* (ESS), within which a client may roam. The ESS identifier is the SSID, which is why the terms *SSID* and *ESSID* are used interchangeably in practice.

The difference between the two names is the cause of many interview slips. The SSID names a service and is shared by many radios; the BSSID names one radio on one band, and it is the address that appears in the frames. A dual-band home router that advertises "Sharma_Home" on 2.4 and 5 GHz has one SSID and two BSSIDs; a campus with 200 APs, three radios each and two SSIDs on every radio has 200 × 3 × 2 = 1,200 BSSIDs. Each BSSID sends its own beacon about ten times a second, so every extra SSID adds management traffic at the lowest data rate. A common rule is to keep the number of SSIDs per radio to three or fewer.

Besides the infrastructure BSS there are three other modes. In an *independent BSS* (IBSS, "ad hoc") stations talk directly with no AP. *Wi-Fi Direct* lets one device act as a software AP for a peer. *Mesh* (802.11s) joins APs wirelessly so that frames are forwarded over several radio hops, which needs the fourth address field ([§6.5](#s6-5){.ref}). In all cases the DS is an abstraction, the "wherever frames go when they leave the BSS", and in practice it is a switched Ethernet network.

## [6.4]{.sec}The MAC: CSMA/CA and DCF timing [Must · ■■□]{.tag} {#s6-4}

Wi-Fi stations share one radio channel, and only one may transmit successfully at a time. Ethernet resolved the same problem with *carrier sense multiple access with collision detection*, in which a sender listens while it transmits and stops when it hears a collision ([§3.3](#s3-3){.ref}). A radio cannot do that. Its own transmission is some 90 dB stronger at its antenna than any signal arriving from afar (100 mW against a received −70 dBm), so a station cannot hear a collision while it sends. Wi-Fi therefore *avoids* collisions, rather than detecting them: this is *CSMA with collision avoidance* (CSMA/CA), and the base procedure is the *distributed coordination function* (DCF).

### Carrier sense and the interframe spaces

A station decides that the medium is busy by two means. *Physical carrier sense*, the clear channel assessment (CCA), declares the channel busy when the radio detects an 802.11 preamble (as weak as about −82 dBm in 20 MHz) or any energy above about −62 dBm. *Virtual carrier sense* uses the *Duration* field present in every frame: a station that overhears a frame sets its *network allocation vector* (NAV), a timer counting down the microseconds for which the medium is reserved, and regards the medium as busy until the NAV reaches zero, even if it cannot hear the other transmitter.

Waiting times are measured in a small set of *interframe spaces*, whose lengths set priorities. The shortest, the *short interframe space* (SIFS), separates a frame from its immediate response (data and ACK, RTS and CTS); the *DCF interframe space* (DIFS = SIFS + 2 slots) is how long a station must find the medium idle before it may contend for access. Because SIFS is shorter than DIFS, the response to a frame always begins before any contender's DIFS has expired. Hence the receiver of a frame gets the medium back without a fight, which is the cause of the ordering rather than a convention.

| PHY | Slot | SIFS | DIFS = SIFS + 2 × slot | CWmin | CWmax |
|----------------------------------------|-------|-------|--------------|-------|-------|
| 802.11a/n/ac/ax in 5 GHz (and 6 GHz) | 9 µs | 16 µs | 34 µs | 15 | 1023 |
| 802.11g/n/ax in 2.4 GHz, short slot | 9 µs | 10 µs | 28 µs | 15 | 1023 |
| 802.11g in 2.4 GHz with 802.11b present | 20 µs | 10 µs | 50 µs | 15 | 1023 |
| 802.11b (DSSS) | 20 µs | 10 µs | 50 µs | 31 | 1023 |

Two more spaces complete the set. The PIFS (SIFS + 1 slot) is used by the legacy point coordination function, and the EIFS (SIFS + the time of an ACK at the lowest rate + DIFS) is used after receiving a corrupted frame, to give the unseen exchange time to finish.

### The DCF algorithm

The procedure for a unicast frame is as follows.

1. If the medium has been idle for DIFS, transmit at once. Otherwise defer.
2. When the medium becomes idle for a DIFS, draw a random integer *backoff* uniformly from [0, CW], where CW starts at CWmin.
3. Count the backoff down by one for each slot in which the medium stays idle. If the medium becomes busy, *freeze* the counter, wait for the medium to be idle for a DIFS again, and resume.
4. When the counter reaches zero, transmit.
5. The receiver, if the frame arrived intact, sends an ACK after a SIFS. If the sender receives no ACK within the timeout, it assumes a collision or loss, doubles the window (CW ← 2·CW + 1, up to CWmax), and returns to step 2. After a success, or after the retry limit (7 for short frames) is exceeded and the frame is dropped, CW returns to CWmin.

The sequence of windows is 15, 31, 63, 127, 255, 511 and 1023 for retries 0 to 6, so after six failures the average wait has grown from 7.5 slots to 511 slots. The random draw is what separates two stations that would otherwise sense the idle medium and start together; doubling the window is what keeps the collision probability from rising as the number of contenders grows. The ACK is necessary precisely because the sender cannot hear a collision: it is the only evidence of success.

The remedy is a short handshake. Before sending a long frame A sends a 20-byte *request to send* (RTS); the AP replies with a 14-byte *clear to send* (CTS), after a SIFS. Both frames carry a Duration field covering the rest of the exchange: the RTS announces 3 SIFS + CTS + DATA + ACK, and the CTS announces 2 SIFS + DATA + ACK. C cannot hear A, but it hears the AP's CTS, sets its NAV for that duration and stays silent. A collision can now occur only on the short RTS, which is cheap to repeat. The price is the extra RTS and CTS airtime, about 100 µs at the lowest rate, so the handshake is applied only to frames above an *RTS threshold*, which is by default so high that it is off.

The mirror image is the *exposed-node problem*. Carrier sense can forbid a transmission that would have caused no harm: in Figure 6.7, C hears B sending to A and defers, although C's frame to D would not have disturbed A. ::: trap
*Claim:* "Wi-Fi uses CSMA/CD like Ethernet", or "RTS/CTS is always on and prevents all collisions." *Why it is wrong:* a radio cannot detect a collision while it transmits, so Wi-Fi uses collision avoidance with a positive acknowledgement; RTS/CTS is applied only above a threshold and merely protects the long frame. *Correct statement:* Wi-Fi is CSMA/CA with ACKs; collisions still occur, but between short RTS frames when the handshake is used.
:::
